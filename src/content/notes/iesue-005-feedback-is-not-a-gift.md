<p class="lead">Consider two comments on a report. “You need to be more careful” tells the writer you&#x27;re unhappy. “The totals in section three don&#x27;t match the appendix; please check them before Friday” gives the writer a problem to investigate.</p>

<p>The first comment may be sincere. The second gives the recipient somewhere to begin. That difference matters more than how courageous the reviewer feels for speaking honestly.</p>

<p>In the Yes-Way, the Weight concerns how we use our ability to help others. Feedback belongs here because it affects another person&#x27;s work. It calls for preparation and follow-through.</p>

<h2 id="what-a-major-review-found">What a major review found</h2>

<p>Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi&#x27;s 1996 meta-analysis brought together 607 estimates of feedback&#x27;s effects, based on 23,663 observations. On average, feedback improved performance, but more than a third of the effects were negative.<a class="citation" href="#note-1" aria-label="Source 1">[1]</a> That isn&#x27;t a claim that one third of workplace conversations cause harm. It is a reason to question the assumption that offering feedback automatically helps.</p>

<p>Their feedback intervention theory examines where a comment directs attention: toward the task, toward learning how to do it, or toward concerns about oneself. Someone busy defending their competence or status may have less attention left for the work. This offers one explanation to consider, rather than a prediction of how every conversation will go.</p>

<p>John Hattie and Helen Timperley&#x27;s review of educational feedback similarly distinguishes feedback about tasks, processes, self-regulation, and the person.<a class="citation" href="#note-2" aria-label="Source 2">[2]</a> A broad label such as “brilliant” or “careless” gives a learner less guidance than information about the next step.</p>

<p>You can be warm and appreciative while giving useful information. The person should still understand what standard matters, where the work falls short, and what they can try next.</p>

<h2 id="how-criticism-is-interpreted">How criticism is interpreted</h2>

<p>People hear feedback in the context of a relationship. A comment from someone who has repeatedly dismissed your work may mean something different from the same comment offered by a trusted colleague.</p>

<p>David Yeager and colleagues studied critical feedback across the racial divide in U.S. schools. In their experiments, communicating high standards alongside confidence that students could meet them improved responses to criticism, including revision.<a class="citation" href="#note-3" aria-label="Source 3">[3]</a> Mistrust was central to the research. Repeating the same words in a different setting doesn&#x27;t guarantee the same result.</p>

<p>Credibility matters. If you say a person can meet the standard, be ready to explain the standard and provide appropriate support. Praise attached to an impossible demand will not make the demand reasonable.</p>

<p>Manuel London and James Smither examine how people&#x27;s willingness to seek and use feedback develops within a broader feedback culture.<a class="citation" href="#note-4" aria-label="Source 4">[4]</a> Before calling someone defensive, consider what previous conversations have taught them to expect.</p>

<h2 id="candor-has-a-purpose">Candor has a purpose</h2>

<p>In the Analects, Confucius advises a friend to offer honest counsel and skillful guidance, but to recognize when further urging won&#x27;t help.<a class="citation" href="#note-5" aria-label="Source 5">[5]</a> Another passage warns that repeated criticism can drive friends apart.<a class="citation" href="#note-6" aria-label="Source 6">[6]</a> Friendship differs from a manager&#x27;s duty to address performance. Still, the question is useful: are you helping the person see a problem, or trying to make them concede?</p>

<p>In Creativity, Inc., Ed Catmull describes Pixar&#x27;s Braintrust, where people offer candid criticism while the director decides what to do with it.<a class="citation" href="#note-7" aria-label="Source 7">[7]</a> This account doesn&#x27;t prove that the arrangement suits every organization. It does offer an example of how authority and ownership shape the use of criticism.</p>

<p>In String Leadership at EntrLabs, developing others is a central responsibility. I judge the value of feedback by what it helps the recipient understand and do. The Lead Letter carries that applied concern into everyday management decisions.</p>

<h2 id="be-direct-without-making-it-personal">Be direct without making it personal</h2>

<p>An observation can be accurate and still be delivered badly. Public embarrassment, judgments about character, and a list of old grievances can make the useful information hard to hear. How you raise the concern matters.</p>

<p>Avoiding the conversation also has a cost. A colleague can&#x27;t meet an expectation you&#x27;ve never made clear. If safety, fairness, or required standards are at stake, you may need to raise the issue again even when the first conversation was unwelcome. Knowing when to pause doesn&#x27;t remove your responsibility to address the problem.</p>

<p>Be specific about the behavior and its consequences. Invite the person&#x27;s account; you may be missing context. Agree on what needs to happen next and what help is available. Where the decision is genuinely open, say so. Where a requirement is fixed, do not disguise it as a choice.</p>

<h2 id="prepare-the-next-conversation">Prepare the next conversation</h2>

<p>Before giving criticism, write down what happened and which standard wasn&#x27;t met. “Three deadlines were missed this month” gives you something to discuss. “You don&#x27;t care” makes an assumption about motive that the missed deadlines alone can&#x27;t establish.</p>

<p>Choose a time when the information can still be used. Explain the concern, then ask how the recipient understands the situation. Work toward a concrete next step rather than a general promise to improve.</p>

<p>Check that you both understand the next step. Asking “What will we do next?” is more useful than demanding immediate gratitude or agreement. Leave room for the other person to question your interpretation.</p>

<p>Use your Weight Log to follow up. Did the work improve? Were expectations clearer? Did you provide the support you promised? One conversation won&#x27;t prove what caused a change, but checking the outcome keeps you responsible for more than delivering a message.</p>

<p>Feedback is part of a working relationship. Its value becomes clearer after the conversation, when someone has to act on it.</p>
