<p class="lead">Ask yourself why a conversation went badly and an answer probably comes quickly. You were tired. The other person misunderstood. You were trying to help. Any of these could be true, but an explanation isn't more accurate just because it comes easily.</p>

<p>Self-reflection matters, especially when it helps us notice feelings we have ignored. The difficulty begins when we treat our account as the final word. We know what we meant to do; other people experience what we actually did. Leadership requires attention to both.</p>

<p>The Mirror in the Yes-Way asks us to check how we see ourselves against how we behave.</p>

<h2 id="an-explanation-is-not-always-an-observation">An explanation is not always an observation</h2>

<p>In their 1977 review, Richard Nisbett and Timothy Wilson examined the limits of people's access to the processes behind their judgments. In one demonstration, shoppers preferred items toward the right of a display of identical stockings, yet explained their choices by describing qualities of the fabric. When asked about position, they generally rejected it as an influence.<a class="citation" id="cite-1-1" href="#note-1" aria-label="Source 1">[1]</a></p>

<p>A person can give a sincere explanation without knowing what caused the choice. Wilson explored this problem further in Strangers to Ourselves.<a class="citation" id="cite-2-1" href="#note-2" aria-label="Source 2">[2]</a> Neither work suggests that we know nothing about ourselves. The point is that confidence alone doesn't make an explanation reliable.</p>

<p>Looking inward can mean different things. Paul Trapnell and Jennifer Campbell distinguished reflection driven by curiosity from rumination associated with threat and distress.<a class="citation" id="cite-3-1" href="#note-3" aria-label="Source 3">[3]</a> Thinking through a difficult experience may help you understand it. Repeating the same accusation against yourself may tell you nothing new.</p>

<aside class="essay-insight" aria-label="An idea to consider"><p>Start with the details you can recall or check. What words did you use? When did the conversation change? What did the other person do next? You still have to interpret what happened, but now you have something specific to examine.</p>

</aside>

<h2 id="what-other-people-can-see">What other people can see</h2>

<p>A story recorded in the Old Book of Tang describes Emperor Taizong's response to the death of his adviser Wei Zheng. Bronze could show whether his clothing was in order; history could illuminate the fortunes of states; other people could reveal his gains and losses. With Wei Zheng gone, he had lost one of these mirrors.<a class="citation" id="cite-4-1" href="#note-4" aria-label="Source 4">[4]</a></p>

<p>An emperor's relationship with an adviser is very different from a modern feedback conversation. Still, the story captures something useful: a person willing to correct you can show you what private reflection misses. The Analects also connects observing others with examining yourself.<a class="citation" id="cite-5-1" href="#note-5" aria-label="Source 5">[5]</a> What you notice in another person can prompt a question about your own conduct.</p>

<p>Robert Burns gave the wish a memorable form in “To a Louse”: to see ourselves as others see us.<a class="citation" id="cite-6-1" href="#note-6" aria-label="Source 6">[6]</a> Adam Smith's impartial spectator offers another way to step outside immediate self-interest and reconsider our conduct.<a class="citation" id="cite-7-1" href="#note-7" aria-label="Source 7">[7]</a> These are different accounts of moral judgment, but each asks us to loosen our grip on our own perspective.</p>

<p>Psychologist Simine Vazire's self–other knowledge asymmetry model helps explain why neither viewpoint is enough on its own. Different observers have access to different information. In her study, people judged their own neuroticism-related traits best, friends judged intellect-related traits best, and neither had an advantage for extraversion-related traits.<a class="citation" id="cite-8-1" href="#note-8" aria-label="Source 8">[8]</a> Another person's judgment gives you evidence to consider, not a final verdict.</p>

<p>Tasha Eurich's applied work makes a related distinction between understanding yourself and understanding how others see you.<a class="citation" id="cite-9-1" href="#note-9" aria-label="Source 9">[9]</a> As a leader, ask where someone else's perspective could fill a gap in your own.</p>

<h2 id="making-honesty-possible">Making honesty possible</h2>

<p>Self-awareness is the first dimension of the String Leadership model I use at EntrLabs. In practice, that means comparing what you intended with what you did, asking how it affected others, and making adjustments. When the accounts don't match, investigate the difference before turning it into a judgment about your character.</p>

<p>This matters most when you have authority. A colleague may know exactly how you respond to disagreement and still hesitate to tell you. An invitation to be honest does little if previous disagreements have brought embarrassment, exclusion, or a difficult performance review.</p>

<p>Make the request specific and let the other person choose whether to answer. “What happened when I disagreed with your proposal?” is easier to address than “Am I a good leader?” Listen before explaining your intentions. If you respond by defending every detail, the next answer will probably contain less information.</p>

<p>There are limits. Constantly asking for reassurance can burden colleagues, and one person's opinion can reflect prejudice, limited contact, or a particular conflict. Look for examples and patterns. You retain responsibility for deciding what the evidence means.</p>

<section class="practice-panel" aria-labelledby="borrow-a-mirror"><h2 id="borrow-a-mirror">Borrow a mirror</h2>

<ol class="practice-steps"><li><p>Choose one behavior that matters to your work, such as how you respond when someone challenges you in a meeting. Write down your current view and a recent example before asking anyone else.</p>

</li><li><p>Ask two or three people who have seen that behavior what you did and what happened afterward. Make it clear that they can decline. Don't press for something they'd rather keep private.</p>

</li><li><p>Compare their accounts with yours. Where do they agree? Where do they differ? Did they see something you missed, or see you in a different setting? Choose one change to try. Over the next month, use your Evening Review to record what you did and what happened, rather than giving yourself a score for self-awareness.</p>

</li></ol><p>These studies examine different situations, from laboratory judgments to personality assessments and leadership. They don't show that others are always right or that reflection is useless. They give us reasons to compare perspectives and check explanations against evidence.</p>

<p>Private discipline, in the Yes-Way, includes questioning the story you'd prefer to believe. The Mirror helps when it reveals something you can act on.</p>

</section>
