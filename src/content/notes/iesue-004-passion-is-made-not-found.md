<p class="lead">A few months into a new role, the excitement has faded. The work takes longer than expected, the easy successes have run out, and a reasonable question begins to sound like a verdict: perhaps this is not my passion.</p>

<p>It might be the wrong role. Difficulty alone can't tell you that, though. You may have reached the stage where learning takes longer than you expected.</p>

<p>In his 2005 Stanford commencement address, Steve Jobs urged graduates to keep looking for work they loved.<a class="citation" id="cite-1-1" href="#note-1" aria-label="Source 1">[1]</a> That is an understandable aspiration. Trouble arises when we expect love of the work to arrive fully formed, before we have learned enough to recognize what is interesting about it.</p>

<p>The Path in the Yes-Way asks you to choose a direction, give it enough time and effort to learn from it, and reconsider as you find out more.</p>

<h2 id="expectations-change-the-experience">Expectations change the experience</h2>

<p>Paul O'Keefe, Carol Dweck, and Gregory Walton studied beliefs about whether interests are fixed or can develop. Across their studies, people with a fixed view were less open to unfamiliar interests and more likely to expect passion to provide plenty of motivation. When engaging material became difficult, interest fell more sharply among participants encouraged to hold a fixed view.<a class="citation" id="cite-2-1" href="#note-2" aria-label="Source 2">[2]</a></p>

<aside class="essay-insight" aria-label="An idea to consider"><p>These experiments can't tell you whether to stay in a particular career. They do suggest a belief worth questioning: if the right subject should always hold your attention, an ordinary challenge can start to look like proof that you chose badly.</p>

</aside>

<p>Patricia Chen, Phoebe Ellsworth, and Norbert Schwarz examined a related distinction in attitudes toward work. People who emphasized finding a fit and those who emphasized developing passion differed in what they expected at the beginning and how they evaluated work possibilities.<a class="citation" id="cite-3-1" href="#note-3" aria-label="Source 3">[3]</a> Wanting meaningful work does not require expecting immediate enjoyment.</p>

<p>Are you losing interest in the work itself, or are you disappointed that it takes effort? Those answers may lead to different decisions.</p>

<h2 id="how-interest-develops">How interest develops</h2>

<p>Suzanne Hidi and K. Ann Renninger describe four phases of interest development: an initial spark, sustained interest in a situation, an emerging personal interest, and a well-developed one.<a class="citation" id="cite-4-1" href="#note-4" aria-label="Source 4">[4]</a> In their model, curiosity can deepen as you learn more, find value in the subject, and have opportunities to return to it.</p>

<p>That progression isn't automatic. Support matters, and an interest can fade. The model explains how interest may develop; it doesn't promise that enough hours will make you love anything.</p>

<p>Getting better at something can help. As you learn, you notice details you once missed and ask questions you couldn't have asked before. The work may become interesting for reasons quite different from the novelty that first drew you in.</p>

<p>Passion also has different qualities. Robert Vallerand and colleagues distinguish harmonious passion, which can coexist with other commitments, from obsessive passion, which involves a more pressured relationship with the activity.<a class="citation" id="cite-5-1" href="#note-5" aria-label="Source 5">[5]</a> Developing an interest slowly does not guarantee that it will be harmonious. Pay attention to whether you can step away, meet other obligations, and choose how the activity fits into your life.</p>

<h2 id="practice-before-ease">Practice before ease</h2>

<p>Aristotle's account of habituation links the development of virtue to repeated action.<a class="citation" id="cite-6-1" href="#note-6" aria-label="Source 6">[6]</a> Confucius describes a long course of personal cultivation in his account of his life, and elsewhere distinguishes knowing, loving, and delighting in something.<a class="citation" id="cite-7-1" href="#note-7" aria-label="Source 7">[7]</a><a class="citation" id="cite-8-1" href="#note-8" aria-label="Source 8">[8]</a></p>

<p>These passages concern moral development, not career choice. They remind us that practice takes time, but they don't establish a scientific sequence that every interest follows.</p>

<p>Cal Newport's So Good They Can't Ignore You argues for building valuable skills as a route toward satisfying work.<a class="citation" id="cite-9-1" href="#note-9" aria-label="Source 9">[9]</a> Its examples offer possibilities to consider, rather than representative evidence of how everyone's career develops.</p>

<p>This matters for HYOH, my career writing for students and early-career readers. Hiking your own hike includes giving a direction a fair trial. Some possibilities only become visible once you've learned enough to pursue them.</p>

<h2 id="a-fair-trial-has-limits">A fair trial has limits</h2>

<p>An employer can misuse this argument by telling an unhappy employee to stay and wait for passion. So can a parent who has selected a child's career. The possibility that interest can develop does not remove the need for choice, decent conditions, or a feasible alternative.</p>

<p>Consider what is actually changing. Are you gaining skill? Does any part of the work make you curious? Are you receiving enough guidance to improve? Is the cost of continuing manageable? An absence of progress may call for better support, a different approach, or a change of direction.</p>

<p>A harmful setting does not become worthwhile because learning is sometimes uncomfortable. Nor must a job become a passion to have value. It may provide stability, useful contribution, or room for commitments elsewhere.</p>

<section class="practice-panel" aria-labelledby="keep-a-record-of-what-changes"><h2 id="keep-a-record-of-what-changes">Keep a record of what changes</h2>

<ol class="practice-steps"><li><p>Choose one interest or direction you're unsure about and set a realistic trial period. Twelve weeks might suit some situations; yours may need more or less time. Decide beforehand what time and support you can afford.</p>

</li><li><p>Each week, record one thing you can now do better, one question that interests you, and something you still find frustrating. If numerical ratings help, use them as personal prompts rather than objective measures of competence or passion.</p>

</li><li><p>Notice when curiosity brings you back without prompting: you return to a problem, look for an explanation, or spot a detail you once missed. Record the costs too, including what the work takes away from other parts of your life.</p>

</li></ol><p>At your next Trail Check, look over the record. If both skill and curiosity are growing, you may have reason to keep going. If your skills improve but your interest doesn't, that matters too. You can continue for other reasons or decide to put your effort elsewhere.</p>

<p>Leave room for interest to develop without postponing a decision forever. Give yourself enough experience to judge the work more fairly than you could during the first difficult week.</p>

</section>

<aside class="carrying-question"><p class="callout-kicker">A question to carry</p><p>Which interest deserves a fair trial, and what would count as evidence that it is growing?</p></aside>
