<p class="lead">Imagine you&#x27;ve spent a year on a project. Colleagues know you backed it. The budget keeps growing, the results haven&#x27;t arrived, and someone asks whether it&#x27;s time to stop.</p>

<p>Two questions can become tangled: Is the project still worth pursuing? And what would stopping say about your judgment?</p>

<p>The second question can drown out the first. Another month buys time before you have to explain a disappointing result. It also costs money, attention, and the chance to do something else. The Path in the Yes-Way asks us to take that choice as seriously as we take persistence.</p>

<h2 id="how-commitment-escalates">How commitment escalates</h2>

<p>In Barry Staw&#x27;s 1976 study, participants made a simulated business investment decision and then received information about its outcome. Those responsible for an earlier decision that had gone badly committed more resources than participants who hadn&#x27;t made that decision.<a class="citation" href="#note-1" aria-label="Source 1">[1]</a></p>

<p>The study illustrates a risk, not an inevitable response to failure. Later research has examined how personal responsibility, social pressure, and features of the project and organization affect decisions to keep investing. Dustin Sleesman and colleagues reviewed these influences in a meta-analysis.<a class="citation" href="#note-2" aria-label="Source 2">[2]</a> A review needs to examine the conditions around a decision as well as the decision maker&#x27;s intentions.</p>

<p>Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer found a more everyday example in theater attendance. People who paid full price for season tickets attended more performances during the first half of the season than people given discounts.<a class="citation" href="#note-3" aria-label="Source 3">[3]</a> What they had already paid influenced what they did next, even though attending couldn&#x27;t recover the money.</p>

<p>Past investment can tell you something useful: what you&#x27;ve learned, which obstacles remain, and whether a different approach might work. But money you cannot recover is not a benefit of continuing. The next investment needs its own justification.</p>

<h2 id="the-difficulty-of-choosing">The difficulty of choosing</h2>

<p>Mencius begins a passage with a choice between fish and bear&#x27;s paws when he cannot have both.<a class="citation" href="#note-4" aria-label="Source 4">[4]</a> He then turns to a much weightier choice between life and righteousness. A project review is not equivalent to that moral choice. Still, the opening names a familiar difficulty: choosing something valuable can mean giving up something else you value.</p>

<p>The Daodejing offers warnings about holding on too long and becoming rigid.<a class="citation" href="#note-5" aria-label="Source 5">[5]</a><a class="citation" href="#note-6" aria-label="Source 6">[6]</a> Epictetus opens the Enchiridion by distinguishing what is within our control from what is not.<a class="citation" href="#note-7" aria-label="Source 7">[7]</a> These are philosophical perspectives, not instructions about which job or project to leave. They invite us to reconsider what we are trying to preserve.</p>

<p>For the decision in front of you, separate what you can change from what you can only regret. You may be able to reduce the scope, try another method, or arrange a handover. Continuing cannot erase what has already been spent.</p>

<h2 id="evidence-for-letting-go">Evidence for letting go</h2>

<p>Carsten Wrosch and colleagues studied what happens when goals become unattainable. The ability to let go of those goals and invest in alternatives was associated with well-being.<a class="citation" href="#note-8" aria-label="Source 8">[8]</a> Much of the evidence was correlational, so it cannot establish that quitting causes a better life. It does give us reason to take goal adjustment seriously.</p>

<p>Steven Levitt studied volunteers who were struggling with major decisions and willing to receive a recommendation from a coin toss. For important decisions, those told to make a change were more likely to change and reported greater happiness six months later.<a class="citation" href="#note-9" aria-label="Source 9">[9]</a> These were people already considering a change; some did not follow the recommendation or complete follow-up. The study cannot decide whether a particular job, project, or relationship is worth leaving.</p>

<p>In Quit, Annie Duke recommends deciding in advance what would make you stop.<a class="citation" href="#note-10" aria-label="Source 10">[10]</a> A criterion chosen before your reputation feels threatened can be easier to use honestly than one invented in the middle of a difficult meeting.</p>

<p>The research on grit also gives us reason to question persistence as an explanation for success. Marcus Credé and colleagues found substantial overlap between grit and conscientiousness and challenged some of the stronger claims made for grit.<a class="citation" href="#note-11" aria-label="Source 11">[11]</a> Their findings do not make effort pointless. They do make “keep going” an incomplete answer.</p>

<h2 id="adaptive-resilience-in-practice">Adaptive resilience in practice</h2>

<p>In String Leadership, adaptive resilience means responding when circumstances or evidence change. HYOH brings that concern to career decisions: hiking your own hike includes checking whether the route still serves a goal you choose.</p>

<p>A hard week is not, by itself, a reason to leave. Iesue 004 explores how interest can grow through learning and why early frustration can mislead us. Give a worthwhile effort enough time and support to develop, and be honest about what that continued effort costs.</p>

<p>Include the people affected by your decision. A project may need a handover, documentation, or a plan for unfinished responsibilities. Leaving professionally means attending to those consequences where it is feasible and safe. It does not mean staying indefinitely because an organization refuses to prepare for your departure.</p>

<h2 id="set-a-review-before-you-need-one">Set a review before you need one</h2>

<p>Choose one important commitment. Write down what you want it to achieve, what you can reasonably invest, and when you will review it. Describe the evidence that would justify continuing, changing the approach, or stopping.</p>

<p>Use criteria that fit the purpose. For a learning project, look for skills you can now demonstrate. For a service, look for evidence that people can use it. Include time, costs, and obligations alongside the measures that make the project look good.</p>

<p>Share your criteria with someone willing to question them. At your Trail Check, a scheduled review of the path you&#x27;re taking, compare those criteria with what has actually happened. Ask what you would choose today if you hadn&#x27;t made the original decision.</p>

<p>You may discover that a criterion was poorly chosen. If you change it, record the new evidence and your reason. Moving the threshold again and again without learning anything is a warning that the review has become a way to defend the decision.</p>

<p>Whether you stay or leave, be able to explain what the next commitment is for and why it deserves the resources you are giving it.</p>

<aside class="carrying-question"><p class="callout-kicker">A question to carry</p><p>If you were choosing today, what would justify another month on this path?</p></aside>
