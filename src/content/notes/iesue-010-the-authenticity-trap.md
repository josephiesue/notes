<p class="lead">Consider a new manager who says, “I&#x27;m not comfortable giving direct feedback.” A researcher avoids presenting unfinished work because uncertainty feels unprofessional. A quiet colleague turns down an opportunity that would require speaking more often.</p>

<p>These may be honest descriptions of how each person feels. But a feeling can become a limit before the person has tried a different approach. “That&#x27;s not me” ends the discussion just when there might be something useful to learn.</p>

<p>The Mirror in the Yes-Way asks us to look at how we act now and what our responsibilities require next. A clear picture of your habits should help you decide what to practice.</p>

<h2 id="a-familiar-piece-of-advice">A familiar piece of advice</h2>

<p>In Hamlet, Polonius tells Laertes, “to thine ownself be true.”<a class="citation" href="#note-1" aria-label="Source 1">[1]</a> The line travels well as advice, but a character&#x27;s speech in a play needs interpretation. It cannot settle what a manager owes a colleague or what a new role will require.</p>

<p>Modern advice about authenticity needs interpretation too. Being honest, keeping a promise, and saying whatever comes to mind are different things. You can acknowledge that you dislike direct feedback and still owe someone a clear account of a problem.</p>

<p>Researchers use authentic leadership in a more specific sense than “do what feels natural.” Walumbwa and colleagues described four components: self-awareness, openness in relationships, balanced consideration of information, and an internal moral perspective.<a class="citation" href="#note-2" aria-label="Source 2">[2]</a> That is a more demanding account than simply expressing a preference.</p>

<h2 id="what-the-research-debate-tells-us">What the research debate tells us</h2>

<p>George Banks and colleagues found substantial overlap between authentic and transformational leadership in their meta-analysis.<a class="citation" href="#note-3" aria-label="Source 3">[3]</a> This raises questions about how much the two research constructs explain separately. It does not show that honesty is unimportant.</p>

<p>Mats Alvesson and Katja Einola also challenged what they saw as excessive positivity in authentic leadership research, questioning its assumptions, measures, and claims.<a class="citation" href="#note-4" aria-label="Source 4">[4]</a> Their critique is part of a research debate, rather than a verdict on every leader who values authenticity.</p>

<p>For a working leader, the debate invites more concrete questions. Are people getting a clear account of the situation? Have I considered evidence against my view? Am I meeting the responsibilities of my role? Feeling like yourself does not answer those questions.</p>

<h2 id="learning-through-provisional-selves">Learning through provisional selves</h2>

<p>Herminia Ibarra studied professionals moving into more senior roles. They observed role models, tried unfamiliar ways of working, and assessed the results. She called these experiments “provisional selves.”<a class="citation" href="#note-5" aria-label="Source 5">[5]</a> Her later essay on the authenticity paradox explains how a fixed idea of who you are can get in the way of that learning.<a class="citation" href="#note-6" aria-label="Source 6">[6]</a></p>

<p>A reserved person can practice opening a meeting. Someone uncomfortable with criticism can learn to describe a problem respectfully. The behavior may feel rehearsed at first because it is being learned. That feeling does not, by itself, make the behavior dishonest.</p>

<p>William Fleeson and Joshua Wilt found that feeling authentic was not simply a matter of behaving in line with one&#x27;s usual personality traits. People could feel authentic while acting differently from their typical patterns.<a class="citation" href="#note-7" aria-label="Source 7">[7]</a> The study concerns a feeling, not a test of moral integrity, but it challenges the idea that familiarity is the only guide to being yourself.</p>

<p>These studies do not tell you which behavior is right in every role. They give you reason to try an unfamiliar approach and assess what it actually does.</p>

<h2 id="practice-and-character">Practice and character</h2>

<p>In Analects 12.1, Confucius links humaneness with self-restraint and a return to li, often translated as ritual propriety.<a class="citation" href="#note-8" aria-label="Source 8">[8]</a> The idea belongs to a broader ethical and social tradition; reducing it to workplace etiquette would miss much of its meaning.</p>

<p>It offers a useful question, though: could practicing a form of conduct help change the person practicing it? Adam Seligman and colleagues explore a related possibility in their account of ritual as an “as if” world of shared forms.<a class="citation" href="#note-9" aria-label="Source 9">[9]</a> This is a way to think about practice, not evidence that a particular leadership exercise works.</p>

<p>In String Leadership at EntrLabs, the concern is how people develop through conduct they can examine and practice. A description of someone&#x27;s current tendencies can inform that work. It should also leave room for change.</p>

<h2 id="what-should-remain-consistent">What should remain consistent</h2>

<p>Experimenting becomes a problem when winning approval is the only measure of success. You can become better at impressing people while doing less to meet your responsibilities.</p>

<p>Keep the distinction between learning a method and creating a false impression. Preparing a clear explanation is useful. Pretending to know what you don&#x27;t know is dishonest. Practicing considerate behavior can help you keep a commitment; claiming a commitment you have no intention of honoring cannot.</p>

<p>The Yes-Way&#x27;s commitments to service, protection, and keeping promises give the practice a purpose. Judge a new behavior by whether it helps you meet those commitments and by its effect on other people.</p>

<h2 id="test-a-behavior-you-have-ruled-out">Test a behavior you have ruled out</h2>

<p>Choose one behavior your role needs that you tend to avoid. Make it specific: end a meeting with agreed responsibilities, ask for clarification when you are unsure, or raise a concern before a deadline.</p>

<p>Watch two people who handle that task well in different ways. Borrow one useful method and try it in a suitable situation. Preparing what to say is part of learning; you don&#x27;t have to pretend it comes easily.</p>

<p>In your Evening Review, a short reflection at the end of the day, record what happened. Did the method help? What felt awkward? Ask a trusted observer whether it made the work clearer or more useful, rather than whether you seemed different.</p>

<p>After several attempts, adapt the method to your own voice or try another. The experiment should give you information, not an obligation to imitate someone indefinitely.</p>

<p>You can keep your commitments while changing how you act on them. Your previous habits are a starting point, not the final word.</p>

<aside class="carrying-question"><p class="callout-kicker">A question to carry</p><p>Which responsibility have you avoided because the required behavior felt unfamiliar?</p></aside>
