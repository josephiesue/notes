<p class="lead">A learner is stuck, and you can see the next step. Giving the answer would take seconds. Deciding whether to give it requires more thought.</p>

<p>Sometimes a direct explanation is exactly what&#x27;s needed. At other times, a hint or a little room to try helps the learner notice something an immediate answer would conceal. Their relief at getting the answer tells you the pressure has eased. It doesn&#x27;t tell you what they can now do.</p>

<p>That matters to the Weight in the Yes-Way: using your ability in ways that leave other people more capable. What kind of help will still be useful when you&#x27;re no longer beside the learner?</p>

<h2 id="what-an-attempt-can-prepare">What an attempt can prepare</h2>

<p>Manu Kapur&#x27;s research on productive failure examines what learners can gain from an unsuccessful attempt. In these learning designs, students tackle a problem before receiving instruction that helps them make sense of it.<a class="citation" href="#note-1" aria-label="Source 1">[1]</a> The value lies in what the attempt prepares them to learn.</p>

<p>Tanmay Sinha and Kapur&#x27;s 2021 review compared that sequence with instruction followed by problem solving. Across 53 studies and 166 comparisons, they found an overall advantage for problem solving first.<a class="citation" href="#note-2" aria-label="Source 2">[2]</a> Results varied with the design and the learners. Some categories, including younger pupils and tasks involving general rather than subject-specific skills, favored instruction first. The review supports a carefully designed sequence, not a rule that everyone should struggle before being taught.</p>

<p>The explanation afterward is essential. It connects learners&#x27; attempts to the concept they need to understand. Assigning a hard task and refusing to help is not enough to make failure productive.</p>

<p>Trying several approaches can give a learner a reason to care about the explanation: they have encountered the problem it solves. But they need enough prior knowledge to make a meaningful attempt. Confusion without a way in can consume the lesson.</p>

<h2 id="difficulty-has-to-serve-learning">Difficulty has to serve learning</h2>

<p>Elizabeth and Robert Bjork describe desirable difficulties: learning conditions that can make practice harder while improving later retention.<a class="citation" href="#note-3" aria-label="Source 3">[3]</a> Spacing practice and recalling information from memory are examples. The difficulty serves a specific learning purpose. Hardship alone does not.</p>

<p>In experiments by Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke, students who practiced recalling material remembered more on delayed tests than students who repeatedly studied it. Repeated study felt reassuring and helped on an immediate test, but that advantage did not last.<a class="citation" href="#note-4" aria-label="Source 4">[4]</a> A smooth lesson can leave both teacher and learner overconfident.</p>

<p>Novices also need guidance. Paul Kirschner, John Sweller, and Richard Clark argued that minimally guided instruction can demand too much of a learner who lacks the relevant knowledge.<a class="citation" href="#note-5" aria-label="Source 5">[5]</a> Their critique is a reason to judge what the learner needs before deciding to hold back an explanation.</p>

<p>David Wood, Jerome Bruner, and Gail Ross described tutoring through the idea of scaffolding: support that helps a learner manage a task they cannot yet complete alone.<a class="citation" href="#note-6" aria-label="Source 6">[6]</a> The useful question is practical. What help makes the task possible now, and what help can you reduce as the learner becomes more capable?</p>

<h2 id="older-teaching-scenes">Older teaching scenes</h2>

<p>In Analects 7.8, Confucius describes showing one corner and expecting the learner to work out the other three.<a class="citation" href="#note-7" aria-label="Source 7">[7]</a> His expectations belong to a different teaching tradition. The passage still raises a useful question for a teacher today: how much room are you leaving for the learner to contribute?</p>

<p>In Plato&#x27;s Meno, Socrates guides an enslaved boy through a geometry problem using questions and mistaken answers.<a class="citation" href="#note-8" aria-label="Source 8">[8]</a> The scene serves a philosophical argument about recollection. It is not an experiment proving that unguided discovery works; Socrates is actively shaping the inquiry.</p>

<p>Both scenes draw attention to the learner&#x27;s thinking. An incorrect attempt can show a teacher what needs explaining. That is the comparison worth carrying into a classroom, without treating an ancient dialogue as modern research evidence.</p>

<h2 id="connecting-the-practice-to-entrsl">Connecting the practice to EntrSL</h2>

<p>EntrSL, the service-learning approach in my work, engages learners with meaningful problems. ELEM examines engagement in relation to learners&#x27; goals and the learning culture around them. Both make the design of challenge and support important.</p>

<p>A real problem can give a learner a reason to care. It can also exceed what they currently know how to do. A task does not become educationally sound just because it is authentic. Community partners and colleagues should not bear avoidable harm for the sake of a learning exercise.</p>

<p>Plan what learners need to know beforehand, what they can explore, and where you will teach directly. If an error could have serious consequences, demonstrate the procedure first or create a protected setting for practice.</p>

<h2 id="staying-present">Staying present</h2>

<p>“Productive failure” can become an excuse for inadequate preparation. If learners cannot get started and receive no useful explanation afterward, the teacher has left them with an obstacle.</p>

<p>Learners bring different knowledge, confidence, language resources, and support. One person may find a task challenging and useful while another cannot see how to begin. Watch what happens and adjust the help. Asking for assistance is not proof that someone hasn&#x27;t tried.</p>

<p>Be careful with stories about your own difficult education. Learning under harsh conditions does not establish that the harshness caused the learning or should be repeated.</p>

<h2 id="try-a-supported-attempt">Try a supported attempt</h2>

<p>Choose a low-risk task that the learner has enough knowledge to attempt. Ask what they have tried and where they are uncertain. If they cannot begin, offer a hint, an example, or a smaller first step.</p>

<p>Give them time to think. Silence may mean they are working through an idea; it may also mean they are lost. Look for signs of reasoning and ask a brief question if you cannot tell. There is no universal waiting time that makes a teacher helpful.</p>

<p>Then explain clearly, using the attempt to show why an approach worked or where it broke down. Give the learner another chance to apply the idea.</p>

<p>Return later with a related problem. In your Weight Log, a record of how your help affects others, note what the learner can do independently and where support is still needed. Later understanding tells you more than relief at the end of a difficult moment.</p>

<p>The teacher&#x27;s work continues through the attempt, the explanation, and the next opportunity to learn. Kindness includes staying attentive enough to know when the help needs to change.</p>

<aside class="carrying-question"><p class="callout-kicker">A question to carry</p><p>For the next learner who asks for help, what would let you see their thinking before deciding how to respond?</p></aside>
