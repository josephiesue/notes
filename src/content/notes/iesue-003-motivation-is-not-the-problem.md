<p class="lead">A student contributes in one class and says almost nothing in another. A colleague offers thoughtful ideas in a small working group but becomes cautious in the department meeting. Calling either person unmotivated leaves an obvious question unanswered: why does the behavior change with the setting?</p>

<p>People bring different interests, goals, skills, and responsibilities to their work. The conditions matter too. If you explain everything through personality, you can miss problems a teacher or leader has the power to fix.</p>

<p>In the Yes-Way, the Ground is the setting in which people learn and work. It includes the tasks they're given, the responses they expect, and what happens when they ask for help.</p>

<h2 id="helping-the-wrong-way">Helping the wrong way</h2>

<p>Mencius tells of a farmer who worried that his plants were growing too slowly. He pulled them upward and returned home exhausted by his efforts. The plants withered.<a class="citation" id="cite-1-1" href="#note-1" aria-label="Source 1">[1]</a></p>

<aside class="essay-insight" aria-label="An idea to consider"><p>Mencius uses the story in an argument about moral cultivation. For a leader, the farmer's mistake is easy to recognize: he worked hard to help without understanding what the plants needed.</p>

</aside>

<p>A motivational speech can make the same mistake when the obstacle is an impossible workload, unclear evaluation, or a history of punishing questions. Encouragement has its place. It cannot repair those conditions by itself.</p>

<p>In “Encouraging Learning,” Xunzi uses the image of a plant growing upright among hemp to draw attention to its surroundings.<a class="citation" id="cite-2-1" href="#note-2" aria-label="Source 2">[2]</a> He and Mencius disagreed about human nature, and neither was writing organizational psychology. Their images still offer a useful question: what are these surroundings encouraging a person to become?</p>

<h2 id="what-the-room-teaches">What the room teaches</h2>

<p>Kurt Lewin expressed the relationship in the familiar formula B = f(P, E): behavior is a function of the person and the environment.<a class="citation" id="cite-3-1" href="#note-3" aria-label="Source 3">[3]</a> The formula does not tell a manager which intervention to choose. It reminds us that the setting belongs in the explanation.</p>

<p>Achievement goal research looks more closely at people's reasons for trying. Elliot and McGregor distinguish mastery goals, which concern competence, from performance goals, which concern doing better than others. Each can involve pursuing success or avoiding failure.<a class="citation" id="cite-4-1" href="#note-4" aria-label="Source 4">[4]</a> Wanting to understand a task, wanting to outperform a colleague, and wanting to avoid looking incompetent can lead someone into the same room for very different reasons.</p>

<p>Carole Ames's review of classroom research examined how tasks, evaluation, and authority can encourage different goal orientations.<a class="citation" id="cite-5-1" href="#note-5" aria-label="Source 5">[5]</a> Public rankings draw attention to comparison. Opportunities to revise work make improvement more visible. These choices communicate a teacher's priorities, even when the teacher says nothing about them.</p>

<p>Workplace learning research asks related questions. The Dimensions of the Learning Organization Questionnaire covers continuous learning, inquiry and dialogue, team learning, systems for sharing learning, empowerment, connection with the environment, and strategic leadership for learning.<a class="citation" id="cite-6-1" href="#note-6" aria-label="Source 6">[6]</a> These are substantive features of a learning culture. A weekly training session cannot stand in for all of them.</p>

<p>The questionnaire, often shortened to DLOQ, records people's perceptions; it isn't an independent check of every workplace practice. Even so, asking whether people feel able to question decisions or share what they've learned tells you more than assuming that quiet people lack ambition.</p>

<h2 id="the-question-behind-elem">The question behind ELEM</h2>

<p>My doctoral work on ELEM, the Entrepreneurial Learning Engagement Model, examines achievement goals and learning culture together. I want to understand how people's reasons for developing competence relate to their participation in a particular learning environment.</p>

<p>That question guides this essay. It doesn't mean motivation matters little or culture determines everything. Those relationships need evidence. ELEM directs attention to both the learner and the conditions in which learning takes place.</p>

<p>Classrooms and workplaces also differ. A finding from one may suggest something worth trying in the other, but it doesn't prove that the same approach will work there.</p>

<h2 id="culture-still-has-owners">Culture still has owners</h2>

<p>Blaming “the culture” can let everyone off the hook. Yet culture is sustained through decisions people make: who gets credit, how mistakes are discussed, what earns a promotion, and whose time is treated as expendable.</p>

<p>Start with the decisions you control. If people are expected to ask for help but are criticized for needing it, the inconsistency deserves attention. If collaboration matters but only individual output earns recognition, people face a conflict that another speech about teamwork will not resolve.</p>

<p>Individuals still have choices, and their circumstances extend beyond the room. A quieter participant may be tired, uncertain, unfamiliar with the language, or simply thinking. Avoid turning every difference into a diagnosis of culture. Ask before interpreting.</p>

<section class="practice-panel" aria-labelledby="conduct-a-small-ground-audit"><h2 id="conduct-a-small-ground-audit">Conduct a small Ground Audit</h2>

<ol class="practice-steps"><li><p>Choose one team or class to observe for a month. Notice where people contribute freely and where they hold back. Invite them to describe the difference privately, without asking them to disclose personal difficulties.</p>

</li><li><p>Write down what the setting rewards or discourages. Look beyond formal assessments. Notice praise, interruptions, jokes, who gets a second chance, and what happens when someone flags a problem.</p>

</li><li><p>Identify one inconsistency between what you say matters and what people experience. Change something within your authority. For example, explain how revisions will be assessed, or make time to discuss an unsuccessful attempt without publicly assigning blame.</p>

</li></ol><p>Watch what follows. Alongside attendance and participation, notice the questions people ask, their requests for help, and the quality of their work. A month of observation won't prove that your change caused an improvement, but it can help you judge whether to continue or adjust it.</p>

<p>The farmer's lesson is hard for people who like to act quickly. Before increasing pressure, find out what's getting in the way. The useful change may be ordinary: clearer instructions, a fairer response to mistakes, or enough time to learn.</p>

</section>

<aside class="carrying-question"><p class="callout-kicker">A question to carry</p><p>What happens in your team or classroom when someone tries, struggles, or asks for help?</p></aside>
